Convergence

Is NVIDIA overvalued, undervalued, or fairly priced right now? Most people have an opinion. Almost nobody can say for certain how they’d know.

That’s because the question assumes there’s a single right answer to find. Convergence starts somewhere different: instead of asking what a company is worth, it asks whether the story the market is pricing and the story the financial structure is telling currently agree — and tracks what happens when they don’t.

Verified example · NVDA · as of 7 August 2026

Convergence reads Central Agreement — PRICE_NORMAL__STRUCTURE_NORMAL. Price behavior and financial-structure behavior both sit in the normal band relative to NVIDIA’s own multi-year history. There isn’t a clean split to resolve on this date; the two stories are reading as aligned. That’s still informative: agreement is an observable state, not only disagreement.

This is observation, not a forecast. Convergence does not tell you what will happen next — or what NVIDIA is “really” worth.

Abstract framing (no company named)

Sometimes a company’s price and its underlying financial structure genuinely disagree — and there’s no reliable way to know in advance which one is “right.” Convergence doesn’t try to force an answer. It observes the disagreement and tracks what happens to it.

Other times, they agree — and that’s worth naming too. Either way, the framework classifies the relationship as it stands today and re-checks it every month.

Classification, not prediction

Convergence is a research framework that classifies how a company’s price behavior and financial-structure behavior relate at a specific point in time — then re-observes that classification every month as new data arrives.

A relationship state describes the relationship between two signals, not whether a stock is “good” or “bad.” It is descriptive — not a buy signal, sell signal, or price target.

Each month, on the 7th, the framework pulls point-in-time data — only information that would actually have been available on that date — and asks: has the relationship changed?

What Convergence gives you: a named description of what is observed now, and a record of how that observation has shifted over time.

What it does not give you: a prediction of what happens next, a valuation, or an investment recommendation.

Most frameworks rush to a verdict. Convergence names the relationship first.

When people talk about companies, the conversation usually collapses quickly: cheap or expensive, buy or sell, bullish or bearish. Convergence takes a different starting point. Before asking “what should someone do,” it asks: what is the state of agreement between price and structure right now — and how has that state changed?

  • A company can look “expensive” on price while its structure reads stable — or the reverse.
  • Two companies in the same industry can share a headline narrative while their price–structure relationships diverge completely.
  • A relationship that looked settled last month may look different this month, without either story having “won.”

Observe. Classify. Track. Test.

Convergence operates in four repeating stages: observe point-in-time data, classify the price–structure relationship, track how that classification changes, and test hypotheses against historical outcomes.

Step 1 — Point-in-time data

Each month, Convergence pulls financial and price data as it would have existed on the observation date — not with hindsight. This keeps the classification honest: it reflects what was knowable then, not what we learned later.

Step 2 — Classify the relationship

The framework compares a company’s recent price behavior against its recent financial-structure behavior — each measured relative to that company’s own multi-year normalized history, not a generic industry average or a simple 1-year price chart. The output is a relationship state: a plain description of how the two signals relate right now.

Step 3 — Track transitions over time

When a relationship state changes, Convergence records the transition. Change is the point. A single snapshot is less informative than the sequence.

Step 4 — Test hypotheses, publish the result

Separately from live classification, Convergence tests specific claims about what certain relationship patterns have historically tended to precede. Each test uses a fixed rule agreed in advance — a hypothesis — scored across thousands of independent historical comparison units. Each unit gets a yes or no: did the pattern behave as the hypothesis predicted over the following ~12 months?

The result is published whether it holds or not.

How to read “unusual” in Convergence. When Convergence says a company’s price is reading “unusually” low or high, it means recent price momentum — how the stock has been moving year over year — looks weak or strong compared to that same company’s own history over many years. That is not the same as where the stock sits on a 1-year chart. A company can trade near recent highs on a standard chart while still reading as unusually weak on price behavior, if its recent momentum is negative relative to its own longer history — and vice versa.

None of this is a return forecast. The tests measure whether a structural pattern showed up in a follow-up window — not whether anyone made money.

The framework running today — in live trading (paper).

Convergence maintains a live trading (paper) application layer — three parallel modes, each tested across three Mix variants (Mix 1, Mix 2, and Mix 3 are different combinations of company types the framework can hold, run side by side for comparison — not a ladder from safer to riskier).

Portfolio A — Structural Filter Starts from a broad list of companies, removes those with active structural red flags, and holds the rest evenly.
Portfolio B — Pure Convergence The framework alone decides among equally eligible candidates — no human input, no AI override.
Portfolio C — Convergence + Human The framework generates the eligible pool and advisory context; a human operator makes the final selection each month — intentional, not a gap in automation.

Scope, stated plainly:

  • This is live trading (paper) only — internal ledgers, simulated execution, not a fund, not investment advice.
  • Holdings update on a monthly cadence (7th of each month); prices refresh on weekdays via the publish pipeline.
  • Portfolio C requires human input for final picks; that step cannot run unattended without defeating its purpose.
  • Price data comes from yfinance (adjusted closes); execution is simulated, not broker-submitted by default.

Descriptive only — not a performance claim. Figures show mark-to-market values since last review, not investment results.
