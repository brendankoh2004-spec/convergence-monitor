Convergence

Every company has two stories.

What the market is pricing. What the financial structure shows. They don’t always agree. Convergence is built to notice that, name it, and track how it changes.

Right now, on Dexcom (DXCM): as of the most recent monthly observation (7 August 2026), the stock price is reading unusually low compared to its own recent history — while the company’s financial structure is reading unusually strong on the same date. One signal says “discounted.” The other says “structurally elevated.” Convergence doesn’t pick a winner between them. It names the disagreement and keeps watching.

You don’t need to know how to read a balance sheet to find that interesting. You only need to recognize that a company can have two public faces — and that those faces can drift apart.

This is observation, not a forecast. Convergence does not tell you what will happen next.

Classification, not prediction.

Convergence is a research framework that classifies how a company’s price behavior and financial-structure behavior relate to each other at a specific point in time — then re-observes that classification every month as new data arrives.

A relationship state is Convergence’s name for that classification. It describes the relationship between two signals, not whether a stock is “good” or “bad.”

Continuing the Dexcom example:

  • Convergence currently reads DXCM as being in a state where price is unusually low while structure is unusually high — a split between the two stories.
  • That label is descriptive. It is not a buy signal, a sell signal, or a price target.

Each month, on the 7th, the framework pulls point-in-time data — only information that would actually have been available on that date — and asks: has the relationship changed? If price and structure move back into alignment, the classification updates. If the gap widens, that gets recorded too.

What Convergence gives you: a named description of what is observed now, and a record of how that observation has shifted over time.

What it does not give you: a prediction of what happens next, a valuation, or an investment recommendation.

Not a buy signal, sell signal, or price target.

Most frameworks rush to a verdict. Convergence names the relationship first.

When people talk about companies, the conversation usually collapses quickly: cheap or expensive, buy or sell, bullish or bearish. To get there, almost every approach quietly picks a side — trust the price, or trust the fundamentals.

Convergence takes a different starting point. Before asking “what should someone do,” it asks: what is the state of agreement between price and structure right now — and how has that state changed?

That matters because the relationship itself carries information that a single verdict can erase:

  • A company can look “expensive” on price while its structure reads stable — or the reverse.
  • Two companies in the same industry can share a headline narrative while their price–structure relationships diverge completely.
  • A relationship that looked settled last month may look different this month, without either story having “won.”

For anyone making high-stakes judgments — in investing, research, or collaboration — the question isn’t only “what do I think?” It’s also “what am I actually observing, and has it shifted?” Convergence is built for that second question. It holds the verdict open long enough to describe what’s going on.

Observe. Classify. Track. Test.

Convergence operates in four repeating stages: observe point-in-time data, classify the price–structure relationship, track how that classification changes, and test hypotheses against historical outcomes.

Step 1 — Point-in-time data

Each month, Convergence pulls financial and price data as it would have existed on the observation date — not with hindsight. This keeps the classification honest: it reflects what was knowable then, not what we learned later.

Step 2 — Classify the relationship

The framework compares a company’s recent price behavior against its recent financial-structure behavior — each measured relative to that company’s own history, not a generic industry average. The output is a relationship state: a plain description of how the two signals relate right now.

Step 3 — Track transitions over time

When a relationship state changes, Convergence records the transition. Change is the point. A single snapshot is less informative than the sequence.

Step 4 — Test hypotheses, publish the result

Separately from live classification, Convergence tests specific claims about what certain relationship patterns have historically tended to precede. Each test uses a fixed rule agreed in advance — a hypothesis — scored across thousands of independent historical comparison units. Each unit gets a yes or no: did the pattern behave as the hypothesis predicted over the following ~12 months?

The result is published whether it holds or not.

None of this is a return forecast. The tests measure whether a structural pattern showed up in a follow-up window — not whether anyone made money.

Six hypotheses. Tested on real historical data. Including the ones that failed.

Investigation D scored six pre-registered principles across independent comparison units in historical data. Below is what actually happened — confirmed and falsified results treated the same way.

Falsified

Principle A

Hypothesis: When two similar companies shared the same relationship type on the same date, the one with fuller internal consistency would reach a better subsequent structural state within ~12 months.

Result: It did not hold. Across 8,234 independent comparison pairs, the pattern appeared roughly 4 times out of 10 — below the 50% coin-flip baseline.

Falsified

Principle B

Hypothesis: Among flagged companies, those with passing forensic integrity would transition more favorably within ~12 months than flagged peers with weaker structural reads.

Result: It did not hold. Across 285 independent cases, the pattern appeared roughly 4 times out of 10.

Confirmed

Principle C

Hypothesis: A company’s current static quality read does not determine the direction of its subsequent structural transition.

Result: It held, barely above chance. Across 330 independent comparisons, the pattern appeared just over half the time — 51%.

Confirmed

Principle D

Hypothesis: A company showing a change-underway structural profile would have a more informative subsequent transition than a peer with a static profile.

Result: It held, just above chance. Across 3,251 independent pairs, the pattern appeared just over half the time — 51%. (One required case — Abbott vs. AMD, July 2022 — was scored individually and answered Yes, as reported in the source file.)

Confirmed

Principle E

Hypothesis: When a company’s relationship type is still unresolved, confidence in reading its subsequent path is capped.

Result: It held clearly. Across 205 independent comparisons, the pattern appeared roughly 7 times out of 10 — 68%.

Confirmed

Principle F

Hypothesis: An active structural fracture signal marks instability.

Result: It held. Across 4,145 independent pairs, the pattern appeared roughly 6 times out of 10 — 61%.

These are tests of structural patterns in historical data — not return forecasts, not proof that any portfolio beat a benchmark, and not a reason to trade. Convergence publishes the failures alongside the confirmations because a framework that only reports what worked isn’t one you can trust.

Why publish falsified results? Because a framework that only reports what worked isn’t one you can trust. Publishing failures shows where the signal ends and noise begins.

The framework running today — on paper.

Convergence maintains a live paper-simulation application layer — three parallel modes, each tested across three Mix variants (Mix 1, Mix 2, and Mix 3 are different combinations of company types the framework can hold, run side by side for comparison — not a ladder from safer to riskier).

Portfolio A — Structural Filter Starts from a broad list of companies, removes those with active structural red flags, and holds the rest evenly.
Portfolio B — Pure Convergence The framework alone decides among equally eligible candidates — no human input, no AI override.
Portfolio C — Convergence + Human The framework generates the eligible pool and advisory context; a human operator makes the final selection each month — intentional, not a gap in automation.

Scope, stated plainly:

  • This is paper simulation only — internal ledgers, no live trading, not a fund, not investment advice.
  • Holdings and classifications update on a monthly cadence (7th of each month).
  • Portfolio C requires human input for final picks; that step cannot run unattended without defeating its purpose.
  • Price data comes from yfinance (adjusted closes); execution is simulated, not broker-submitted by default.
